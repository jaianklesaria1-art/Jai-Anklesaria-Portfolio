01 · Context

A luxury business with no website

Singh Vanity rents luxury vanity vans for film sets, concerts, celebrity appearances and weddings, in Mumbai and across India. When the owner came to me, the business had no website at all.

That was a problem in two directions. Production houses compare suppliers quickly, and a business with nothing to show online looks smaller than it is. At the same time, the owner wanted to reach a new kind of customer: people booking a van for their own wedding or family function, who have never hired one before and don't know what to ask for.

So the site had two jobs. It had to convince professional buyers that the vans are worth their price, and it had to make booking simple enough for a first-timer. The owner also asked for one specific feature: a scroll effect that takes visitors inside the van.

Problem statement

How might we make a luxury rental feel premium to film productions, while making booking easy for someone who has never hired a vanity van?

Who the site serves

Five groups book vans, with different needs and very different levels of experience.

Film & ad productions

Need vans on set for cast and crew, often at short notice.

Celebrity teams

Expect privacy, comfort and a van that suits the star.

Event organisers

Need backstage space for concerts, shows and festivals.

Wedding planners

Book a van as a getting-ready room for the bride or groom.

Everyday customers

Booking for their own wedding or function, possibly for the first time.

Goals

  • Credibility: give the business a professional presence that production houses take seriously.
  • Accessibility: make booking easy for everyday customers, not only for people who know the industry.
  • Experience: show what the vans feel like inside, through the scroll effect the owner asked for.

Constraints

  • No existing site or brand system to build from; only the owner's photos of the vans.
  • No booking system. Enquiries had to go to WhatsApp and email, where the owner already works.
  • Prices vary by date, duration and location, so they can't be published as fixed numbers.
  • A heavy effect. The scroll story needs many large images, which is hard on phones.

What the research told me

Four patterns stood out. Each one became a design direction.

Booking is a conversation

Every site ended in WhatsApp or a phone call. Customers want to discuss dates and locations before they commit.

→ WhatsApp is one tap away, with the message already written

Specs don't sell a luxury van

Most sites described vans by door count and colour. None showed what it feels like to step inside.

→ The home page opens by walking the visitor through the van's door

People book for different occasions

The clearest site grouped vans by occasion, so a wedding family and a film crew each found their use.

→ A "Where Singh Vanity goes" section, including weddings

First-timers don't know the vocabulary

"Double door" means something to a production manager, not to a family planning a wedding.

→ The form offers "Not sure / Need recommendation"

03 · Design

From sketch to a scroll through the door

With the research done, I sketched the page structure in Figma before any visual design.

The order follows the questions a visitor has. First, what is this and is it premium? The scroll story answers that. Then which van do I need? (the fleet), is it for someone like me? (the occasions), how does booking work? (four steps), can I trust them? (reviews) and finally how do I get in touch? (contact and WhatsApp).

Low-fidelity structure, redrawn for this case study from my Figma sketches.

Making the scroll effect

The owner asked for a scroll effect that takes visitors inside the van. A 3D model would have been expensive to make and heavy to load, so I built it from a generated video instead: the visitor's scroll plays the video forwards and backwards.

Step 1

Google Flow

Generated a short video that moves from the van on a studio set, through the door, into the lounge.

Step 2

ezgif

Split the video into 26 still frames at 1920 × 1080.

Step 3

GSAP ScrollTrigger

Draws one frame per scroll position on a canvas, so the visitor moves the camera by scrolling.

Step 4

Text panels

Five short lines appear along the way, from "Step inside" to "Every comfort on wheels".

The finished scroll story

04 · Design decisions

Eight decisions that shaped the site

Each decision below is on the live site. For each one, I've set out the reasoning behind it and, where there was one, what it cost.

01

Content hierarchy

Show the inside before the spec sheet

The home page opens with the scroll story, not a list of vans.

Why
Competitor sites described vans by door count and colour. For a luxury service, the interior is what people pay for, so the site shows it first.
Trade-off
26 full-size frames are heavy. On phones, the story starts once six frames have loaded, and the canvas matches the screen's pixel density so it stays sharp.
02

Responsive design

Keep the story on phones

Five alternatives were tried on phones before the scroll story came back.

What I did
Each version went live so the client could check it on his own phone: a poster photo, a text-only opening, a two-photo stack, a van cut-out, and the van on a gold glow. The final version brings the desktop story to phones, with a blurred copy of each frame filling the portrait screen.
Why
Every static version lost what makes the site different. The story is the brand's signature, so it belongs on the device most people use.
Trade-off
The zoom crops the van's edges, and a one-time note suggests desktop for the full view.
03

Conversion

Booking should feel like sending a message

A WhatsApp button on every screen opens a chat with the enquiry already written.

Why
Every competitor ended in WhatsApp or a call, because customers want to talk before committing. A pre-written message removes the hardest part: knowing what to say to a business.
Later change
The button first pulsed. I replaced the pulse with a still button and a soft shadow, a calmer treatment for a luxury brand.
04

Forms

A form that works for first-timers

"Not sure / Need recommendation" sits next to the van types.

What I did
The enquiry form lists the van types by name, plus a full event package and a "Not sure" option. It asks for the event date and location up front, and "How it works" above it explains the four steps from enquiry to event day.
Why
A production manager knows what a triple-door van is; a family planning a wedding doesn't. Saying "I'm not sure" still lets them enquire, and date and location are what the team needs to quote.
05

Information architecture

Name the fleet the way the trade does

Four van types, each with its three standout features.

What I did
Celebrity, Mobile Toilet Van, Double Door and Triple Door, each card with features like Hollywood mirrors or two luxury rooms, a "Book Now" button and "Call for price".
Why
Door-count names are how productions already order vans, so crews recognise them instantly. Prices depend on dates, duration and location, so a fixed number would often be wrong.
Trade-off
Everyday customers get no price anchor. The promise of a tailored quote in "How it works" softens that.
06

Audience

One site for film sets and family weddings

"Where Singh Vanity goes" lets each audience find its occasion.

What I did
Four occasion cards: films and OTT, artists and celebrities, major events, and weddings, marked "Now available". Each cycles through its own photos.
Why
The client wanted everyday customers as well as productions. Grouping by occasion lets a wedding family see themselves on a site that also serves film sets.
07

Mobile patterns

Phones get their own patterns

A navigation bar that scrolls away, and reviews as swipeable cards.

What I did
On phones, the navigation bar scrolls away with the page. Desktop reviews run in three auto-scrolling columns; on phones they become a stack of cards you swipe through.
Why
A pinned bar takes space from the story on a small screen, and three moving columns are unreadable on a phone. One card at a time is easy to read and control.
08

Navigation

"Back" returns you to where you were

Leaving for the gallery or the full story page doesn't lose your place.

Why
The home page is long, with the scroll story at the top. Being sent back to the top would mean scrolling through the whole story again to reach the fleet or the form, so the site remembers the visitor's position and returns them to it.

Reflection

What I learned

1

Agree what "good" looks like before making variants

The mobile hero went through six versions, each pushed live for the owner to judge on his phone. Reviewing on a real device was valuable, but without criteria agreed up front, each round was a new opinion. Next time I'd set two or three tests first, such as "the van is recognisable" and "the booking button is visible without scrolling", and judge every version against them.

2

Design the signature feature for phones first

The scroll story was designed on desktop and adapted for phones afterwards, which caused most of the mobile rework. Building it for a portrait screen first would have saved several rounds.

3

Check every piece of content before launch

Trust matters most for a luxury service, and reviews and credentials carry that trust. Every review and client name should be confirmed with the owner before it goes live.

06 · Outcome

Live at singhvanity.com

The site is live on the owner's own domain, and he loved it. Booking works the way he wanted: one tap opens WhatsApp with the message written, or one short form sends the details by email.

That feedback is qualitative. I don't have enquiry numbers yet, so I'm not claiming a measurable result.

Given more time

I'd track WhatsApp taps and form submissions to see which audiences enquire, and from which section. I'd collect reviews directly from clients, with their permission and the production or event named, and export the scroll frames at a higher resolution.

The mobile hero was chosen through reviews with the client. I'd test it with a few real customers to see whether the story helps or slows them down on the way to booking.
