Marquee uPVC Architectural Fins

How I turned a façade manufacturer's 60-page print catalogue into a website that architects, contractors and developers use to shortlist Marquee and send an enquiry.

Client: Marquee, Mumbai Year: 2026 Role: Research, UX/UI, AI-assisted build Live site: marqueefins.com

01Level 1 · ChallengeMarquee case study · Jai Anklesaria

01

The challenge

Marquee has manufactured uPVC fins and louvers in Mumbai since 2008. Its buyers are architects, contractors and developers, and its fins are on hospitals, schools and residential towers across India. Its website had stopped working.

The old site had been down for a long time. When the client approached me, the only material they had was a 60-page print catalogue from 2020: a cover, an intro page, around 45 full-page photos of finished buildings, one page of profile dimensions and a quality policy. The content an architect needs was all there, but locked in a PDF with no search, no filtering and no way to ask for a quote.

My brief was to turn that catalogue into a website that helps specifiers shortlist Marquee and makes it easy to start a conversation.

Audience

Architects, contractors and developers choosing a façade supplier

Scope

Home page (story, products, advantages, specs, projects, enquiry) and a full Projects page. Desktop and mobile.

The brief

A 2020 print catalogue. No working site, no written brief.

Marquee installations, from the client's own catalogue.

The approach

I started with a competitor audit of Indian façade manufacturers, then broke the catalogue down into content, deciding what each audience needs first and what can wait.

Each stage used a different tool. A Claude research skill found competitors and ran the audit. I designed some components in Figma and took others from 21st.dev. Claude built the site, Google Flow generated the hero film, and Formspree sends every enquiry to the client's inbox.

What the research told me

Lead with installations

The competitors that felt credible showed finished buildings; the weakest relied on stock imagery. For a façade product, installed work is the strongest trust signal.

→ Six signature projects on the home page, all 45 on a Projects page

Put specs where specifiers look

Competitors listed sizes in paragraphs, sidebars or PDFs. Someone comparing profiles needs shapes and dimensions visible without a download (Nielsen heuristic #6, recognition rather than recall).

→ Page 57 of the catalogue became a Profile Dimensions table on the home page

One route to an enquiry

Competitors spread contact across pop-ups, sidebars and chat buttons, or offered none. Every extra route is one more decision before the action that matters.

→ One enquiry form, sorted by type (quotation, technical, samples, partnership), sent to the client's inbox

A working site is a trust signal

Two of the manufacturer sites I checked failed to load or showed a security warning. In this category, a fast, working, well-kept site already sets a supplier apart.

→ Real project photos, 500+ / 200+ / 5 decades up front, and a counterfeit warning

Assumptions to validate

The design rests on assumptions I drew from the catalogue and the audit, not from talking to Marquee's buyers. I haven't validated them yet. This is the research plan I'd run next, with the open question behind each assumption and how I'd answer it.

The design

Two principles guided the design: show the product through finished buildings, and answer the questions a specifier asks before shortlisting a supplier, in the order they ask them.

The interface is near-black so building photography stands out, with the copper from the catalogue's wordmark reserved for accents and calls to action. Headings use Outfit, a geometric sans that suits an architectural brand, and body copy uses Inter for legibility at small sizes.

05Level 5 · OutcomeMarquee case study · Jai Anklesaria

05

The outcome

Marquee is back online on its own domain, marqueefins.com, after years without a working site, and every enquiry now lands in the client's inbox.

The 60-page catalogue now works as a website: the story in three stats, products grouped by purpose, a spec table, 45 projects to browse and a form that sorts each enquiry by what the visitor needs.

What I learned

In B2B work the brief is often hidden in material the client already has. Reading the catalogue as research data showed me what specifiers need: proof, specs and a direct way to get in touch. The audit showed how rarely manufacturers offer all three.

Given more time

I'd set up analytics and track form submissions to measure how many visits turn into enquiries. I'd add the city, product and profile used to each project, show each profile installed rather than only as a line drawing, fix the Projects page so it loads when opened directly from a link, add the two missing project photos, and offer the dimensions as a downloadable spec sheet. Most of all, I'd interview a few of the architects Marquee works with, to test these decisions with the people they were made for.
