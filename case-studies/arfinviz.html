Research case study · MSc HCI dissertation · University of Nottingham × KPMG

Does a 3D chart beat a spreadsheet?

Finance teams at KPMG read complex data in Excel and static 2D charts. For my MSc dissertation I studied whether augmented reality could help them understand that data better, and designed and tested an AR app, ARFinViz, to find out.

01 · The research question

Is 3D actually better, or just newer?

KPMG stakeholders wanted a more immersive way to present financial data. Before designing anything, I needed to know whether a third dimension helps people understand data, or whether it only looks impressive.

Main question

Can augmented reality help finance professionals understand and discuss financial data better than the static 2D tools they use today?

  1. What makes their current tools (Excel, SAP Ariba, static charts) hard to work with?
  2. Which tasks, if any, would 3D and AR help with?
  3. What would stop people from trusting or adopting an AR tool at work?
02 · Method

Four stages, from reading to testing

A mixed-methods study: qualitative interviews to understand the problem, then usability testing and an A/B comparison to evaluate the design.

Stage 1

Literature review

White papers and research on immersive data visualisation, to decide between AR and VR.

Stage 2

Interviews

A 3-week interview sprint with 20+ people who work with financial data.

Stage 3

Synthesis

Themes, How Might We questions, storyboards, MoSCoW prioritisation and a Lean UX canvas.

Stage 4

Design and evaluate

Two design iterations, in-person usability tests, tap heatmaps and an A/B test of onboarding.

From themes to scenarios

I turned the themes into a 10-scene storyboard of a KPMG team meeting: scanning a QR code, finding a surface, exploring data together, and hitting problems. It gave stakeholders a concrete picture of the idea before any screens existed.

05 · Decision log

Five decisions, and what they cost

Each decision traces back to the research. For each one: the evidence, the options, what I chose, and the trade-off.

01

Phone-based AR, not a VR headset

Evidence
Stakeholders asked for an immersive experience. The literature review compared AR and VR on ergonomics and ease of use.
Options
VR headset · AR on phones and tablets · improve 2D dashboards
Decision
AR on the phones people already carry.
Why
No extra hardware. People stay aware of the room and each other, which matters in a meeting.
Trade-off
A small screen and less immersion than VR. AR also depends on finding a flat surface.
02

A learning section, because most people had never used AR

Evidence
Most participants knew AR only from games and had little hands-on experience. They asked for AR-guided tutorials.
Options
One onboarding carousel · tooltips only · a dedicated learning area
Decision
A Learning tab with short lessons, videos and quick quizzes, plus onboarding.
Why
People can't judge a tool they don't know how to use. Learning had to be part of the product.
Trade-off
An extra tab in the navigation, and more content to keep up to date.
03

Collaboration built in, not added later

Evidence
Current tools offered little real-time collaboration, and participants valued seeing the same data together.
Options
Share exports by email · shared sessions with a schedule · full multi-user AR
Decision
Scheduled shared sessions, visible cursors in the AR view, and in-app messaging in version 2.
Why
The storyboards showed AR as a meeting tool. Designing it for one person would miss its main use.
Trade-off
More complex to build, and it needs everyone in a session to have the app.
04

Secure sign-in in version 2

Evidence
Participants said financial data needs password protection and secure handling.
Options
Open access inside the KPMG network · simple login · login with OTP and password recovery
Decision
A full sign-in flow: sign up, log in, OTP verification and password reset.
Why
People won't put client data into a tool they don't trust.
Trade-off
More steps before people see any data. That made onboarding the thing to test in the A/B comparison.
05

Leave 3D maps and personal support for later

Evidence
The MoSCoW board: data visualisation, file management and profiles were essential for testing the core idea.
Options
Build everything in the prototype · build the core and defer the rest
Decision
Core features first. The QR scanner and tutorial came next. 3D maps and personalised support were deferred.
Why
The research question was about reading data. Extra features would have made the test harder to interpret.
Trade-off
3D maps would have suited the tax geography finding. It stays an open idea.

06 · Usability testing

Five tasks, from sign-up to reading data in AR

I ran in-person sessions with 5 to 10 people per round. Each person worked through the whole journey while I observed and took notes.

  1. Account setupSign up and create an account.
  2. Explore data in ARPlace a visualisation and navigate it.
  3. CollaborateUse the shared session features.
  4. Real scenarioAnswer a financial analysis question using the app.
  5. FeedbackTalk through the experience and suggest changes.

What I changed after testing

Better tutorials

Interactive tutorials so people learn the AR controls by using them.

Simpler navigation

Clearer movement between sections, which people found hard to follow.

Guidance in context

Tooltips and short prompts on complex features, at the moment they're needed.

A feedback loop

A way to collect feedback inside the app, for the next round of changes.

A/B test: two versions of onboarding

I compared onboarding in version 1 and version 2 of the app, looking at how easily people got through it and how comfortable they felt. Version 2 performed better.

With 5 to 10 people, one person can shift a percentage by 10 to 20 points, so I treat this result as a direction, not as statistical evidence.

07 · Back to the question

So, is 3D better?

Probably.Not proven

Participants preferred working with data in 3D and found it more engaging than their spreadsheets. But most of them had very little experience with AR, so part of that preference may be novelty. The study shows that AR is promising for presenting and discussing financial data. It doesn't prove that people understand data better in 3D.

Limitations

LimitationWhy it matters
Small samples5 to 10 people per test round is enough to find usability problems, but too few for reliable numbers.
Novelty effectPeople new to AR may like it because it's new. That can inflate preference for 3D.
Who took partParticipants came through KPMG and the university. People who agree to try AR may already be more open to it.
Tap heatmaps, not eye-trackingThey show where people tapped, not what they looked at or understood.
No direct 2D vs 3D task testPeople didn't do the same task in both formats, so accuracy and speed weren't compared head to head.

What I'd do differently

  1. Test 2D and 3D head to headGive each participant the same data tasks in both formats, in a counterbalanced order, and measure accuracy and time.
  2. Use standard measuresAdd the System Usability Scale and NASA-TLX for workload, so results can be compared with other studies.
  3. Reduce the novelty effectRun repeated sessions over a few weeks and see whether the preference for 3D holds.
  4. Plan the sample sizeDecide the number of participants in advance for the comparison, rather than reusing the usability test group.
08 · What I learned

A good question beats a good-looking answer

The most useful thing I did was question the brief. Stakeholders wanted "immersive". The research made me ask whether immersive meant clearer, and the honest answer was "sometimes, and we can't be sure yet".

It also taught me to separate what people like from what helps them. That difference now shapes how I plan every study: decide what you need to measure before you build anything.
