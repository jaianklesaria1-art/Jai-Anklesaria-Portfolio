Neon Raptor Brewing Co.

Redesigning a Nottingham brewery's website so that ordering beer online feels like walking into their taproom.

Client: Neon Raptor Brewing Co., Nottingham Year: 2025 Role: Research, UX/UI, AI-assisted build Live site: neonrapterbrewery.netlify.app
9:41

01

The challenge

Neon Raptor is an independent brewery in Sneinton Market, Nottingham, with its own bar next door, the Rap Taproom. Their brand is a whole world: retro arcade, synthwave neon, beers named like video game levels. Their website did not feel like any of that.

The old site was a Shopify store. On a first visit, two stacked modals, a cookie consent banner and an 18+ age check, covered the page before visitors saw a single beer. The homepage was a promo banner, a "New in" grid and a footer. The neon palms and retro grid, the most recognisable part of the brand, appeared only as decoration at the very bottom of the page, and the homepage said nothing about the taproom.

The brewery wanted a site that sells beer easily and feels like being in their taproom. My job was to work out what that means for a website, design it, and get it built and live.

My role

Research, information architecture, UI design, building the site with AI tools, deployment

Scope

Home, Rap Taproom, Shop with cart and checkout, Info and FAQs. Desktop and mobile.

Process

Research and sketching first, then an AI-assisted build

02

The approach

I started with a competitor scan of UK and US taproom breweries that sell online, then audited the brewery's existing site. From those findings I sketched the information architecture on paper and in Figma, and only then started building.

I used a different tool at each stage. Perplexity sped up the research. Pen, paper and Figma were for working out the structure. Claude Code and Google AI Studio wrote the code, while I made the design decisions and reviewed everything they produced.

What the research told me

The taproom is the product

The strongest brewery sites sell the bar as much as the beer, with visit buttons, venue photos, opening hours and events.

→ Rap Taproom gets its own place in the primary navigation and a secondary CTA in the hero

Keep people in the browsing flow

Adding beer straight from the product grid keeps people shopping. The old site put four controls on every card (size, minus, plus, add to cart), which added a decision to every product.

→ One "+" per card. It adds the beer without opening the cart, and a count on the cart button confirms it

Browse the way the brewery thinks

Collection tiles mirror how breweries organise their beer, by series and range.

→ A collections sidebar in Neon Raptor's own range names (Nielsen heuristic #2, match between the system and the real world)

Put the brand into the experience

Almost every site looked like a tidy store. Neon Raptor's arcade world set them apart, and the old site kept it in the footer.

→ Interactive neon grid, a peelable logo sticker, neon type throughout

03

The design

I worked to two principles: make the site feel like the taproom at night, and keep the path to buying beer short and obvious.

The interface is dark like the bar, and neon pink carries the actions and highlights. Behind every page runs a live neon grid that tilts with the cursor and cycles through four colour moods. Headings use Rajdhani, a condensed display face that reads like an arcade cabinet, and body copy uses Inter for legibility.

04

How I used AI

Each stage had its own tool and a clear job for it. The tools produced the research summaries and the code. I set the direction, made the design decisions and reviewed their output, which often meant catching and fixing what they got wrong.

Research
PerplexityResearching how UK and US taproom breweries sell beer online: how their shops are organised, how they handle age checks, and how they promote their taprooms and events.

Surfaced the patterns on the research board: the taproom as a destination, adding beer straight from the grid, and shopping by collection.

Brainstorming
Pen & paper + FigmaSketching the page structure and content order, then first layout ideas for Home, Rap Taproom, Shop and Info.

Settled the four-page structure and the two entry points on the homepage before any code was written.

Components
React BitsChoosing ready-made interactive components that fit the brewery's retro arcade feel: Grid Scan, Sticker Peel and Circular Gallery.

These became the neon grid background, the peelable logo sticker and the curved photo gallery.

Build
Google AI StudioBuilding the React and TypeScript site from the brewery's real content, then refining it over 27 prompts: the shop, cart and checkout, the background, and the header layout.

By default the cart drawer opened on every add, which broke the browsing flow. I changed it so the beer is added, the cart button shows the new count, and the drawer opens only when you tap the cart (Nielsen heuristic #1, visibility of system status).

Build
Claude CodeWorked directly in the site's codebase alongside AI Studio.

Used with AI Studio to finish the site and prepare it for launch.

Assets & shipping
ImageKit, Netlify + SupabaseImageKit serves the brewery's photos and can images. Netlify hosts the site. Supabase stores the products the shop sells.

Live on Netlify, waiting to move onto the brewery's own domain.

05

The outcome

The site is live on Netlify, with the brewery's products served from Supabase, and it's waiting to move onto their own domain.

What the brewery reported back

The team started using it and loved it. The feedback from customers ordering through it came down to two things: how easy it is to order beer, and how much it feels like being in the taproom.

Feeling like the taproom was the original brief.

What I learned

AI took the project from research to a working shop in days. It has no taste or context of its own, so every useful result came from knowing what I wanted before I asked and spotting quickly when it went wrong. That made the research more valuable, because it gave me clear decisions to direct the build with.

Given more time

I'd add age verification into checkout, which matters for selling alcohol online in the UK. I'd photograph every beer, because some products in the shop still share one can image. And once the site is on its own domain, I'd set up analytics to see where people drop off between the product grid and payment, then test the checkout with real customers in the taproom.
