01 Context

A regular's view of the problem

Liquid Light is the brewery I go to most. I'm in the taproom most weekends, and I got to know the owner there.

The taproom runs rock and metal gigs, makers markets and a pizza night every Sunday, and the brewery sells its beer to drinkers and to pubs. The old website reflected none of that. The brand felt generic, and the three most common tasks were hard to complete.

I showed the owner the problems I'd found and proposed a new site that matched the brewery's name and made those tasks easy. He agreed.

Drinkers

Browse the current range and order cans for delivery or taproom collection.

Pubs & restaurants

Order kegs and casks, or open a trade account if they're a new venue.

Regulars

See what's on today and what's coming up, so they can plan a visit.

02 Audit

Three tasks, four websites

I completed each task on the old site and recorded every page, link and domain along the way.

The audit focused on task flow: where a journey slowed down, reached a dead end or sent the visitor to another website. I left out visual issues and standard patterns such as the age check.

Findings

  1. Purchasing is split across four domains

    The beer list is on Untappd, cans and tickets on a separate Shopify store, and trade orders on Breww. Every task leaves the site at least once, and each destination has its own design and navigation.

  2. Trade customers have no clear entry point

    Existing accounts are sent to Breww. New venues face two unlinked pages, three competing buttons and a form that doesn't load.

  3. The events page can't answer "what's on?"

    Event details are locked inside images, past and future events are mixed together, and recurring events sit in a separate section. The page had fallen out of date.

  4. Enquiries depend on choosing the right inbox

    Visitors choose between sales@, enquiries@ and taproom@ before they can ask a question. Brewery tours can only be booked by email.

04 Design and decisions

From structure to screens

I started with low-fidelity layouts to settle the information architecture: what each visitor needs to see first, and in what order.

Low-fidelity wireframes, redrawn for this case study.

Visual direction from the first build

The owner's main request was a site that matched the brewery's name. These were the visual and interaction decisions in the first build.

Motion as brand: a liquid light show

The site uses a black background with an animated plasma shader that cycles through six colours every 20 seconds.

Why: a liquid light show is coloured oil projected onto a wall during a gig. The background recreates that effect, so the page itself carries the brand.

A logo-led hero

The hero shows only the logo and "Est. 2017", with a looping line of outlined text beneath it: fresh cans, taproom, psychedelic vibes, music, art.

Why: the logo is already familiar from the cans and the taproom mural. The looping text works like an illuminated gig sign and states what the brewery offers in one line.

A persistent primary action

"Buy Beer" stays in the header on every page and is the only filled button in the navigation.

Why: buying beer is the site's main commercial goal. A persistent, visually distinct call to action keeps it one tap away from any page.

Product imagery as the hero

Cans sit directly on the black background with a soft shadow, with name, style and ABV below. There are no card containers, and phones show two products per row.

Why: the can artwork is the brand's strongest asset. Removing containers reduces visual noise, and a two-column mobile grid keeps each label large enough to recognise.

An explorable gallery

Taproom photos are mapped onto a dome that visitors drag to rotate, with a link to the brewery's Instagram.

Why: people choose a taproom for its atmosphere. A draggable dome gives a sense of the space that a flat grid doesn't.

A cursor that fits the concept

On desktop, the pointer becomes a small glowing projector that trails the mouse. It's turned off on touch devices.

Why: it adds a moment of play that fits the light-show concept, and it only appears on devices that have a cursor.

Decision log: preparing for launch

Before the domain switch, I reviewed the first build against the three tasks and made six decisions. Each record shows the problem, the options I considered, what I chose, and what it costs.

Decision 01 · Information architecture

Keep one entry point, and hand off only at payment

Problem
Buying beer or trade stock meant moving between four domains, each with its own navigation.
Options
  • Rebuild the shop and trade ordering inside the new site
  • Link out to each store, as the old site did
  • Keep browsing and the basket on the new site, and hand off to Shopify only at payment
Decision
Browsing, the basket and the trade entry point live on the new site. At checkout, one button passes the basket to the brewery's existing Shopify checkout.
Why
Visitors stay in one navigation system until the moment they pay. The brewery keeps stock, delivery, collection and trade accounts in the tools it already uses.
Trade-off
The checkout looks different from the site. The button label "Continue to payment" and the note "Secure checkout on liquidlightbrewco.shop" set that expectation before the switch.

Decision 02 · Content model

Treat events as structured data, not images

Problem
Event details existed only inside poster images, and the page had fallen out of date.
Options
  • Keep the posters and add a date caption to each
  • Embed the taproom's Instagram feed
  • A dated list with time, line-up and entry, keeping posters for past events
Decision
Each event is one entry with a date, time, line-up and entry price. The list sorts by date and shows a status: On now, Today or upcoming. Past events move to a poster archive automatically.
Why
A text list can be scanned, sorted and read by screen readers. The status label answers the most common question, "is anything on today?", so visitors don't have to work it out.
Trade-off
Someone has to enter each event. One line per event in a single file, with automatic archiving, keeps that effort small.

Decision 03 · Empty state

Design for weeks with no gigs announced

Problem
Some weeks have no gigs announced. An empty list suggests nothing happens at the taproom.
Options
  • Hide the events section until a dated event exists
  • Always show recurring events, then explain the gap and offer a next step
Decision
The weekly Pizza & Pint always appears. Opening hours sit beside the list with today highlighted, and a short message says no new gigs are announced, with a link to the taproom's Instagram.
Why
An empty state should explain itself and offer a next step. Opening hours and a live "open now" status give regulars a reason to visit even without a gig.
Trade-off
The fallback relies on Instagram, which the site doesn't control. It covers the gap, but the event list stays the main source.

Decision 04 · Navigation

One trade entry point, split by account status

Problem
Trade customers had three routes, two unlinked pages and a form that didn't load.
Options
  • Add "Trade" to the main navigation as its own page
  • Put a trade link in the footer
  • A trade banner at the top of the shop, with one action for existing accounts and one for new venues
Decision
The shop opens with a banner for pubs, bars and restaurants. "Trade store" goes to Breww for existing accounts. "Open a trade account" opens the contact form with Trade already selected.
Why
Venue buyers look for ordering where the products are. Splitting the entry by account status gives each group one clear next step. I expected most visitors to be consumers, so the main navigation stays focused on them.
Trade-off
Trade sits one level below the main navigation. The consumer-majority assumption needs testing; if trade traffic is higher than expected, trade moves into the main navigation.

Decision 05 · Forms

Route enquiries by topic, not by inbox

Problem
Visitors had to choose between three email addresses before they could ask anything.
Options
  • List all three addresses with a note on what each is for
  • Build a separate form for each type of enquiry
  • One form with a topic field that changes the prompt
Decision
One form with four topics: general, trade and wholesale, events and taproom hire, and brewery tours. Each topic changes the placeholder text to ask for the details the brewery needs, such as venue name and formats for trade.
Why
Visitors describe what they need instead of learning how the brewery sorts its email. Topic-specific prompts mean fewer follow-up emails before the brewery can reply properly.
Trade-off
All messages arrive in one place. The topic is included in every submission so they're quick to sort.

Decision 06 · Content

Launch with real content only

Problem
The first build contained placeholder products, prices and events, and a card payment form that sent nothing.
Options
  • Launch as it was and replace the content later
  • Replace every placeholder with the brewery's real data before launch
Decision
Products, prices and can images come from the brewery's Shopify store. Events, opening hours and the address come from its previous site. Anything I couldn't verify was removed, including gift sets, clothing, mini kegs and a quiz night.
Why
Placeholder content erodes trust quickly, especially on a page that asks for payment details.
Trade-off
The shop is smaller than the first build suggested. It now matches what the brewery actually sells.

05 Build

How it was built

App

React and Vite, started in Google AI Studio, hosted on Netlify.

Components

Plasma, DomeGallery, GooeyNav, CurvedLoop and ScrollFloat from React Bits, restyled for the brand.

Audit and data

Claude helped me run the audit and connect real data: products, prices and can art from the brewery's Shopify store, and its event listings.

Forms and checkout

Netlify Forms handles the contact form. A Shopify cart link passes the basket to the brewery's checkout.

AI sped up the audit and the build. The priorities, trade-offs and final decisions were agreed between me and the owner.

06 Status and next steps

Live, with the domain switch next

The site is live on Netlify. The brewery's domain, liquidlightbrewco.com, moves over from the old Wix site soon.

It's too early to report results. After three months, I'll review these measures with the owner:

Next steps
